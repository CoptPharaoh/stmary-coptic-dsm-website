The Ecumenical Councils

Defending the Apostolic Faith Through the Centuries

Throughout Christian history, bishops gathered to address theological controversies, preserve the apostolic faith, and clarify the Church's teaching. The Coptic Orthodox Church recognizes Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381), and Ephesus (431): three Ecumenical Councils defending the Holy Trinity and the Incarnation.

Chalcedon (451) is a major historical turning point, but is not accepted as Ecumenical by the Coptic Orthodox Church.

Faith Received, Faith Defended

What Is an Ecumenical Council?

An Ecumenical Council is a gathering of bishops recognized by the Church as having authority to address doctrine affecting the universal Christian faith. Serious disagreements called for a shared examination of Holy Scripture, apostolic Tradition, and the faith inherited in the Church.

The councils did not create new divine truths. They clarified and defended the faith already received from the Apostles. The Coptic Orthodox Church recognizes three such councils.

Preserve Apostolic Teaching

Receive and hand on the faith entrusted to the Church, rather than replace it with a new invention.

Address Theological Controversies

Examine disputed teachings that affect how Christians confess God and understand salvation.

Clarify Orthodox Doctrine

Use careful language to protect the truth witnessed in Scripture and lived in the Church.

Protect the Unity of the Church

Seek a common confession that sustains the Church's worship, teaching, and communion.

An Apostolic Example: Acts 15

At Jerusalem, the Apostles and elders gathered to resolve a dispute about Gentile believers. Acts 15 gives an apostolic example of communal discernment. This apostolic council is distinct from the later historical Ecumenical Councils and is not counted as one of the three.

First Ecumenical Council

The First Ecumenical Council of Nicaea

Recognized by the Coptic Orthodox Church

Year
AD 325
Location
Nicaea, Bithynia (present-day İznik, Türkiye)
Convened by
Emperor Constantine I
Central controversy
Arianism

Arius, a presbyter associated with Alexandria, taught that the Son did not share the Father's eternal existence and was not fully divine. The controversy reached the heart of Christian worship: could the Church worship Christ as true God if He were a creature?

The bishops defended the apostolic confession of the eternal Son. They affirmed that He is begotten, not made, and of one essence with the Father. Nicaea did not invent Christ's divinity; it gave precise expression to the faith already received in Scripture, worship, and the life of the Church.

What Arius Taught

Arius placed the Son on the side of created existence rather than confessing Him as eternally divine with the Father. This was more than a disagreement over a word: it changed the understanding of who Christ is.

What the Council Affirmed

The Son is truly God, eternally begotten of the Father. His divine life is not a gift received as a creature, nor did He begin to exist at a moment in time.

What Homoousios Means

Homoousios means “of one essence” or “consubstantial.” It protects the confession that the Father and the Son share the one divine essence, without confusing their distinct Persons.

Why Nicaea Matters Today

The Savior who reveals the Father and restores us to communion with God is the eternal divine Son, not merely an exalted creature. Our prayer to Christ rests on this confession.

People at the Council

St. Alexander of Alexandria

As bishop of Alexandria, Alexander opposed Arius and defended the eternal divinity of the Son.

St. Athanasius the Apostolic

Athanasius attended as a deacon accompanying Bishop Alexander. He was not yet Pope or Patriarch of Alexandria. He later became one of the most influential defenders of the Nicene faith.

Arius

The Alexandrian presbyter whose teaching about the Son brought the doctrinal dispute into sharp focus.

Emperor Constantine I

Constantine convened the council. The doctrinal deliberations belonged to the bishops; imperial sponsorship did not make the emperor the source of the Church's faith.

The Council's Legacy

Nicaea produced the original Nicene Creed, affirming the full divinity of the Son. The fuller Creed commonly confessed today is the Niceno-Constantinopolitan form traditionally associated with Constantinople in AD 381. These related historical forms should not be treated as identical texts.

Second Ecumenical Council

The Second Ecumenical Council: Constantinople

Recognized by the Coptic Orthodox Church

Year
AD 381
Location
Constantinople
Convened by
Emperor Theodosius I
Central controversy
Continuing Arian disputes and denial of the Holy Spirit's full divinity

Questions about the Trinity continued after Nicaea. Alongside disputes over the Son, some teachings denied or diminished the divinity of the Holy Spirit. Constantinople reaffirmed the Nicene faith and defended the confession of the Spirit as fully divine.

The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, traditionally associated with this council, names the Holy Spirit as Lord and Giver of Life. The Church does not confess three gods: the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are three distinct divine Persons sharing the one undivided divine essence.

The Divinity of the Holy Spirit

The Spirit is not a created messenger or an impersonal force. He is worshiped and glorified with the Father and the Son and gives life to the Church.

One Essence, Three Persons

The Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Spirit. Their distinction does not divide the one God into separate beings or unequal degrees of divinity.

The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed

The Creed joins our confession of the Father and Son to the full confession of the Holy Spirit, the Church, Baptism, the resurrection, and the life to come.

Why Constantinople Matters Today

Baptism, prayer, and the Divine Liturgy are Trinitarian. We receive the Spirit's gifts and glorify the one God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Fathers Who Served the Church

St. Gregory the Theologian

His teaching and leadership helped articulate the Church's confession of the Trinity amid difficult disputes.

St. Gregory of Nyssa

A major defender of Nicene theology whose work explained the unity of the divine essence and the distinction of the Persons.

St. Meletius of Antioch

Meletius presided during the opening stage of the council before his death.

St. Nectarius of Constantinople

Nectarius succeeded Gregory in the see of Constantinople and presided during the later proceedings.

St. Basil's Influence, Not Attendance

St. Basil the Great died in AD 379 and did not attend the council of AD 381. His writings, especially his defense of the Holy Spirit, nevertheless deeply influenced Trinitarian theology.

Third Ecumenical Council

The Third Ecumenical Council: Ephesus

Recognized by the Coptic Orthodox Church

Year
AD 431
Location
Ephesus
Convened by
Emperor Theodosius II
Central controversy
The Nestorian controversy

The controversy concerned how to speak faithfully about the divinity and humanity of Jesus Christ. Nestorius of Constantinople opposed certain uses of the title Theotokos for St. Mary and preferred language that his opponents considered inadequate to express the unity of Christ.

St. Cyril of Alexandria defended the confession that the One born of the Virgin is the eternal Word of God incarnate. Ephesus affirmed the unity of Christ and supported the title Theotokos. The dispute was therefore not only about a title for Mary: it concerned the identity of her Son.

Theotokos: God-bearer

Theotokos means “God-bearer” or “Mother of God.” It does not mean that St. Mary is the origin of the divine nature. The Person she bore according to His humanity is truly God the Word incarnate.

One Lord Jesus Christ

The divine Word truly became human. We do not divide His divinity and humanity into two separate persons, one divine and another human, loosely joined together.

Cyril's defense of the unity of Christ stands at the center of the Coptic Orthodox understanding of the Incarnation. His letters and theological work insist that the same eternal Son truly lived a human life for our salvation.

AD 451 · Historical Turning Point

The Council of Chalcedon: A Turning Point in Christian History

Not accepted as Ecumenical by the Coptic Orthodox Church

Year
AD 451
Location
Chalcedon
Convened by
Emperor Marcian
Central controversy
How to describe the union of divinity and humanity in Christ

Chalcedon met amid continuing disagreements about the Incarnation. Its definition spoke of Christ being acknowledged “in two natures.” Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic Churches accept Chalcedon as Ecumenical; the Coptic Orthodox Church and the broader Oriental Orthodox tradition do not.

The Coptic tradition continued to emphasize St. Cyril's confession of the one incarnate Word. Its rejection of Chalcedon must not be mistaken for a rejection of Christ's complete humanity. The Coptic Orthodox Church confesses Him as fully divine and fully human.

The Cyrillian Confession

“One Incarnate Nature of God the Word” expresses the unity of the incarnate Christ. His humanity is real and complete; it does not disappear into His divinity.

A Complex Historical Separation

Theological and terminological disputes developed alongside ecclesiastical and political conflicts. The separation unfolded over time and cannot responsibly be reduced to a slogan or a single person's actions.

Understanding Other Traditions Fairly

Chalcedonian Christians also intend to protect Christ's full divinity and humanity. Explaining the Coptic position does not require accusing other Churches of denying the Incarnation.

Dialogue and Reception

Modern agreements have clarified areas of common faith. They do not, by themselves, resolve every historical or ecclesiastical question or restore full sacramental communion.

Do Not Confuse Unity with Absorption

The union in Christ is without confusion, alteration, division, or separation. Coptic Orthodox teaching rejects the idea that His humanity was diminished or absorbed. The next section explains why Miaphysitism is not Eutychian Monophysitism.

The Incarnation

Understanding Coptic Orthodox Christology

Miaphysitism Is Not Eutychian Monophysitism

The Coptic Orthodox Church follows the Christological tradition associated with St. Cyril of Alexandria. It confesses the one incarnate Christ, fully divine and fully human. The union preserves the fullness of both divinity and humanity without confusion, alteration, division, or separation.

The Coptic Confession

Coptic Orthodox Miaphysite Christology

  • One incarnate Christ, the eternal Word made flesh.
  • Fully divine, sharing the Father's divine essence.
  • Fully human, with a real human body and rational soul.
  • A union without confusion or separation.
  • Grounded in the Cyrillian tradition.

A Teaching the Church Rejects

Eutychian Monophysitism

Eutychian teaching has historically been associated with an understanding in which Christ's humanity is diminished or absorbed in the union.

The Coptic Orthodox Church rejects this understanding. The Savior truly shares our humanity; His human life is not an appearance.

Why Words Need Care

“Monophysite” is historically controversial and often misleading when used as an unqualified label for the Coptic Church. It can suggest the very denial of full humanity that the Church rejects. Different traditions have used different meanings for theological terms; modern dialogue has found substantial common ground, but not every historical disagreement was merely semantic.

The Alexandrian Tradition

St. Cyril of Alexandria: Defender of the Unity of Christ

SC

The Word Truly Became Human

One Incarnate Nature of God the Word

For St. Cyril, Jesus Christ is not a divine person merely associated with a separate human person. He is the one eternal Word who truly became human. The subject of His divine and human actions is the same Son.

The Word Became Flesh

The Incarnation is real, not a disguise. The Word takes our humanity as His own without ceasing to be God.

The Unity of Christ

The One who works divine wonders is also the One who hungers, suffers in the flesh, and rises from the dead. We confess one Christ, not two sons.

The Title Theotokos

Mary's title protects the identity of the One born of her. The eternal Son is the very same Person who is born in time according to His humanity.

Salvation through the Incarnation

God does not save us from a distance. The Word truly enters our human life, conquers death, and restores our communion with God. The unity of Christ therefore matters for the reality of our salvation.

Modern Theological Dialogue

Toward Greater Understanding

In the modern era, representatives of Oriental Orthodox, Eastern Orthodox, and Roman Catholic Churches have engaged in dialogue about the historical Christological controversies. These encounters have identified substantial common ground in confessing Jesus Christ as fully divine and fully human.

Dialogue has also helped explain how terminology and differing historical receptions contributed to misunderstanding. Careful listening is not the same as pretending that every doctrinal or ecclesiastical question has already been settled.

1973 · Coptic–Catholic Declaration

The joint declaration of Pope Paul VI and Pope Shenouda III expresses a common confession of Christ's divinity and humanity. It is an important primary document of interchurch dialogue, not a declaration that full communion has been restored. Read the joint declaration.

1989–1990 · Orthodox Agreed Statements

Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox representatives articulated shared Christological convictions. The official Coptic Church's record of the 2024 meeting includes these historical agreed statements and later proposals. Read the official Coptic record.

Agreement Is Not Automatically Communion

Full sacramental communion between the Oriental Orthodox and Eastern Orthodox Churches has not been restored simply through theological agreements. Reception by the Churches and remaining ecclesiastical and theological questions require further work. The same caution applies when reading Coptic–Catholic dialogue documents.

The Vatican document is cited as a primary record of a joint declaration, not as an authority defining Coptic Orthodox doctrine.

Questions & Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

01How many Ecumenical Councils does the Coptic Orthodox Church recognize?

Three: Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381), and Ephesus (431). Chalcedon is studied as a major historical council but is not accepted as Ecumenical by the Coptic Orthodox Church.

02Did Nicaea invent the divinity of Jesus Christ?

No. The council defended and clarified the Church's inherited confession of Christ's divinity in response to Arianism. Its precise doctrinal language served the apostolic faith rather than replacing it.

03Why is St. Athanasius important?

He became one of the leading defenders of Nicene Christianity and the full divinity of Christ. At Nicaea he was a deacon accompanying St. Alexander, not yet Patriarch of Alexandria.

04What does Theotokos mean?

God-bearer or Mother of God. It affirms that the Person born of St. Mary according to His humanity is truly God the Word incarnate; it does not make Mary the source of His divine nature.

05Does the Coptic Orthodox Church believe Christ was fully human?

Yes. The Church confesses Christ as fully divine and fully human. His humanity is not diminished, absorbed, or merely apparent.

06Does the Coptic Orthodox Church accept Chalcedon?

No. It recognizes the first three Ecumenical Councils but does not accept Chalcedon as Ecumenical. Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic Churches receive Chalcedon differently.

07Is Miaphysitism the same as Eutychian Monophysitism?

No. Coptic Orthodox Miaphysite Christology confesses the full divinity and full humanity of the one incarnate Christ. It rejects the diminution or absorption of His humanity associated with Eutychian teaching.

08Are Oriental Orthodox and Eastern Orthodox Churches in full communion?

No. Theological dialogues have identified important areas of agreement, but full ecclesiastical and sacramental communion has not been restored.

Faith in Daily Life

Why These Councils Still Matter

The councils were not simply political meetings or abstract debates. They addressed questions at the heart of the Christian life: Who is Jesus Christ? Is He truly God? Did the Word truly become human? Who is the Holy Spirit? How do we confess the one God revealed as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit?

Our Worship

We worship Christ as true God and glorify the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Creed gives words to the faith we live in the Divine Liturgy.

Our Prayer

Christian prayer is communion with the living God, not an exercise in abstract ideas. The identity of the One to whom we pray matters.

Our Salvation

The eternal Word truly shares our humanity to heal it and conquer death. The Church's confession of the Incarnation safeguards our hope in His saving love.

Our Witness

Knowing the faith helps us explain it with clarity, humility, and charity. Faithfulness to the Church need not become hostility toward other Christians.

The Coptic Orthodox Church continues to confess the apostolic faith articulated by the first three Ecumenical Councils. Their teaching belongs not only in history books, but in the worship, prayer, and life of every generation.
